# Question 11: Accuracy Drop After Deployment: Distribution Shift

**Topics:** Distribution Shift, Generalization, Model Deployment, Medical Data

## 📝 Question

> A disease-diagnosis model has 95% accuracy on hospital A's data. After deployment at hospital B, accuracy drops to 78%. What is the most likely cause?

## 🔢 Options

**Option 1:** The model was not complex enough for this problem, and enlarging the architecture will make up for the drop at hospital B.

**Option 2:** Such a drop is usually normal when facing new data, and there is no need to investigate the cause or take corrective action.

**Option 3:** The statistical characteristics of hospital B's data (type of devices, patient population, recording methods) differ from the training data, and the model has not encountered them.

**Option 4:** Training on hospital A's data stopped too early, and continuing training and re-tuning the learning rate will fix the problem.

## 💡 About this question

This question tests **distribution shift / domain shift**. Models assume future data comes from the same distribution as the training data. When the environment changes (another hospital with different devices, patient populations and recording methods), that assumption breaks and performance drops.

Key evidence: accuracy on hospital A (the training distribution) is 95%; so the model performs well on familiar data and the problem is not insufficient capacity or training.

## 🔍 Detailed review of every option

### Option 1 — ❌ Incorrect

> The model was not complex enough for this problem, and enlarging the architecture will make up for the drop at hospital B.

**Incorrect.** The model reaches 95% on hospital A, so its capacity was sufficient for the problem. Enlarging the model does not solve the mismatch between B's data and the training data and may even increase overfitting.

### Option 2 — ❌ Incorrect

> Such a drop is usually normal when facing new data, and there is no need to investigate the cause or take corrective action.

**Incorrect.** A 17-point drop in a medical application may have serious consequences and should not be considered "normal." Such a drop is a **red flag** and requires investigating the cause (comparing data distributions, error analysis) and corrective action (collecting data from B, fine-tuning, domain adaptation).

### Option 3 — ✅ Correct

> The statistical characteristics of hospital B's data (type of devices, patient population, recording methods) differ from the training data, and the model has not encountered them.

**Correct.** Differences in devices, patient populations and recording methods mean that B's statistical distribution differs from the training data and the model has not seen these patterns. This is distribution shift, and it is the most likely explanation for a clear drop after moving to a new environment.

### Option 4 — ❌ Incorrect

> Training on hospital A's data stopped too early, and continuing training and re-tuning the learning rate will fix the problem.

**Incorrect.** If training had stopped too early, accuracy on hospital A's own data would also be low, yet it is 95%. Continuing to train on A's data does not fix the mismatch with B.

## ✅ Correct answer

**Option 3**

> The statistical characteristics of hospital B's data (type of devices, patient population, recording methods) differ from the training data, and the model has not encountered them.

## 🎯 Why it is correct

The model is excellent on familiar data (A) and weak on unfamiliar data (B). That pattern points to a "distribution mismatch," not to capacity or training. Option 3 says exactly this.

## 📌 Key takeaway

Before deploying in a new environment, evaluate and monitor the model on data from that environment; accuracy on training-like data does not guarantee performance everywhere.
