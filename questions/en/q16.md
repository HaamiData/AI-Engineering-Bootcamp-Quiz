# Question 16: Why Does an LLM Fabricate Scientific Citations?

**Topics:** Hallucination, Nature of Language Models, Fact-Checking, External Sources

## 📝 Question

> Why might an LLM produce a completely fabricated but highly convincing scientific citation?

## 🔢 Options

**Option 1:** The model is trained to produce likely text, not to verify truth; without an external source it makes no distinction between ‘sounding plausible’ and ‘being correct’.

**Option 2:** The Temperature in language models is always set high, which makes the details of the citation be chosen randomly.

**Option 3:** The model errs only when it is not connected to the internet, and once connected to the web, all its citations become completely correct.

**Option 4:** Scientific citations were not present in the training data at all, and in the absence of data the model has no choice but to generate random, unstructured text.

## 💡 About this question

This question tests the *nature* of language models: they are trained to predict the next **likely** token, not to check whether something is true or false. A scientific citation follows a very specific pattern (author name, year, title, journal); the model knows this "template" well and can compose one that looks entirely real even if no such paper exists.

## 🔍 Detailed review of every option

### Option 1 — ✅ Correct

> The model is trained to produce likely text, not to verify truth; without an external source it makes no distinction between ‘sounding plausible’ and ‘being correct’.

**Correct.** The training objective is to produce likely, fluent text, not to verify truth. Without access to an external source (search, a paper database), the model does not distinguish between "seeming right" and "being right"; the result is a fabricated yet highly convincing citation. To reduce it, use retrieval from real sources (RAG) and verify citations.

### Option 2 — ❌ Incorrect

> The Temperature in language models is always set high, which makes the details of the citation be chosen randomly.

**Incorrect.** Temperature is not always high; a moderate setting is common and many applications set it low. Hallucination occurs even at temperature zero, because its root is the training objective and the lack of verification, not just sampling randomness.

### Option 3 — ❌ Incorrect

> The model errs only when it is not connected to the internet, and once connected to the web, all its citations become completely correct.

**Incorrect.** Web access (search or tools) can reduce errors significantly, but it does not make "all citations completely correct": the wrong source may be retrieved or summarized incorrectly, or the model may still fabricate. Absolute words like "all" and "completely" signal a false claim.

### Option 4 — ❌ Incorrect

> Scientific citations were not present in the training data at all, and in the absence of data the model has no choice but to generate random, unstructured text.

**Incorrect.** Scientific citations are abundant in training data; that is exactly why the model learned their template so well. The fabricated output is also not "random and unstructured"; it is fully structured and plausible, which is what makes it dangerous.

## ✅ Correct answer

**Option 1**

> The model is trained to produce likely text, not to verify truth; without an external source it makes no distinction between ‘sounding plausible’ and ‘being correct’.

## 🎯 Why it is correct

The model is a "likely-text writer," not a "truth writer." Without a verifiable external source, fluent output is not necessarily correct.

## 📌 Key takeaway

Fluent output ≠ correct output. Verify important citations and claims against real sources.
